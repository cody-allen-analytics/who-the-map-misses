    Ethics & Indigenous data sovereignty¶

    Resumen en español

    Este proyecto evalúa si un mapa de pobreza elaborado con aprendizaje automático (el Índice de Riqueza Relativa de Meta) subestima la pobreza de las comunidades indígenas de México, comparándolo con el Censo de Población y Vivienda 2020 del INEGI, y cuánto costaría ese error en la distribución de recursos públicos. Solo se publican resultados agregados: ninguna tabla identifica localidades y los mapas muestran patrones regionales, no comunidades. Las comunidades estudiadas no participaron en el diseño de esta investigación. Si usted pertenece a una comunidad afectada, o colabora con una, y tiene observaciones u objeciones, puede abrir un issue en GitHub o escribir al autor (los datos de contacto están en CITATION.cff). Cada solicitud recibe respuesta y queda registrada en esta página.

    Studying the algorithm, not the communities¶

    This project audits a tool and the institutions that use it. It does not build a new instrument for profiling Indigenous communities, and it does not publish rankings of specific localities as candidates for or against programs. When the map errs, the finding is about the map and the decisions built on it, not about the communities it describes.

    Whose data these are¶

    Indigenous data sovereignty is the right of Indigenous peoples to govern data about their peoples, lands and resources: how the data are collected, who uses them, for what, and to whose benefit. It follows from the right to self-determination recognized in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, including the rights to take part in decisions that affect them (Article 18) and to shape the economic and social programs that affect them (Article 23); in ILO Convention 169, which Mexico ratified in 1990 and which gives Indigenous peoples a part in formulating, implementing and evaluating development plans and programs that affect them (Article 7); and in Article 2 of Mexico's Constitution.

    Every input to this project is data about Indigenous peoples and their lands that they did not govern:

    Data Produced by Indigenous participation in this use
    Census results by locality (ITER) INEGI, under Mexico's statistical law, for official statistics None: this is a secondary use of census data
    Relative Wealth Index Meta and university partners, from household surveys, satellite imagery and connectivity data None
    AlphaEarth satellite embeddings Google and Google DeepMind, from Earth observation data None
    Rank errors, model predictions and results This project None so far

    The last row is the one this project controls. Its locality-level layer (rank errors for every map tile, and the challenger models' deprivation predictions for every rural locality) would amount to a new map of Indigenous communities made without them. The publication rules below keep that layer unpublished.

    The CARE principles: what this project does, and where it falls short¶

    The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019) complement FAIR data practice with principles about people and purpose. Listing only what a project does well would misrepresent it, so each row also says where this one falls short.

    Principle What this project does Where it falls short
    Collective benefit: data ecosystems designed so Indigenous peoples benefit Asks whether a public tool under-serves Indigenous communities and what that would cost them in a budget formula (H6). The remedy it points to is better governance of maps, not more mapping of Indigenous communities. Indigenous communities did not choose this question, and any benefit depends on agencies acting on the results.
    Authority to control: Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in data recognized, and their authority to control them Publishes only aggregates; locality-level data stay on the analyst's computer. Affected communities can ask for a result to be corrected, given context or withdrawn, and their requests take precedence over the pre-analysis plan. No Indigenous community or organization governs this project or approved this use. The census categories are the state's, not the communities' own.
    Responsibility: sharing how data are used, building relationships, respecting Indigenous languages and worldviews Methods, code and the pre-registered plan are public, with a summary in Spanish. Findings are framed as map and institutional error. There is no relationship with affected communities yet and nothing in Indigenous languages. The three poverty definitions measure deprivation from outside, not Indigenous conceptions of well-being.
    Ethics: Indigenous rights and well-being first, at every stage of the data life cycle No individual or household data, aggregates only, maps aggregated to regions, and out-of-scope uses stated in the model card. Group-level findings can still stigmatize, and because the inputs and code are public, anyone can rebuild the locality-level layer this project declines to publish.

    Publication rules¶

    These rules are part of the pre-registered plan (§10) and are enforced in code where possible.

    1. Aggregates only. Published tables are broken down by group, decile, locality size or groups of municipalities. No table may carry a locality or map-tile identifier or coordinates: mapaudit.publication checks this in the end-to-end test on every push and in the release check.
    2. Regions, not communities, on maps. Map tiles are averaged into hexagons about 40 km across, weighted by population, and a hexagon holding fewer than five tiles is not drawn.
    3. No named communities in results. Spot checks against INEGI's tables are run locally, and their output is cleared before notebooks are published.
    4. No locality-level predictions. The challenger models' predictions for individual localities are never published: they would be a new deprivation map of Indigenous communities made without their consent, which is what this project questions.
    5. Synthetic results are labeled as synthetic wherever they appear.

    Who counts as Indigenous here¶

    The analysis classifies places, not people. A locality counts as Indigenous when at least 40% of its residents live in what INEGI calls Indigenous census households, a classification based on the languages spoken in the household. The 40% cut-off follows the convention of Mexico's federal Indigenous institute (INPI, formerly CDI). Thresholds of 30% and 50%, and a measure based on individual speakers of Indigenous languages, are robustness checks.

    None of these measures is self-identification, which Mexico's Constitution makes the fundamental criterion for who is Indigenous (Article 2). Language-based measures miss people who identify as Indigenous but do not speak an Indigenous language, and all of them treat dozens of distinct peoples, speaking languages from 68 recognized language groups, as a single category. Results therefore describe places classified by census measures, never people's identities.

    Language¶

    • "Indigenous" is capitalized; specific peoples are named where the data allow.
    • Deprivation is described as a lack of services the state should provide, not as a trait of a community.
    • Results are reported in whichever direction they go, including if the map turns out to overstate Indigenous deprivation.

    Corrections, objections and withdrawal¶

    If you belong to, or work with, a community affected by this analysis and see a problem with how it is represented, or object to its being studied at all, please open an issue or write to the author (contact details in CITATION.cff). Every request is answered and logged below. Requests from affected communities take precedence over the pre-analysis plan: a result can be corrected, given context or withdrawn, and the change is also recorded in the plan's deviations log.

    What would make this work better¶

    • Co-design. Deciding with Indigenous organizations which definition of poverty to audit against, which errors matter most, and how results are shared.
    • Returning results. Plain-language summaries in Spanish and, with partners, in Indigenous languages, shared with organizations in the regions most affected.
    • Community-governed data. Statistics produced and governed by Indigenous communities as the ground truth, instead of census categories or corporate maps.
    • Open to collaborate. A Local Contexts Open to Collaborate Notice, telling communities that the project is open to working with them on how their data are used.
